# Document anatomies

*The shape each document type must take. The lead element comes first, the thing the reader gets even if they stop after ten seconds.*

## Board / risk-committee memo (pre-read)
**Lead:** the ask, in one sentence, then the risk position in two. **Anatomy:** ask → risk position (what exposure exists today, quantified) → recommendation with price → alternatives considered in one line each, including status quo → evidence of safety/control (metrics, tests, audit posture) → what you're asking the committee to note vs. approve. **Ceiling:** 1–2 pages + appendices. **Register note:** committees read pre-reads before the meeting and challenge in it, so every claim needs a defensible source because the challenge is the point of the body. Write to survive the sharpest member, not the median. **Failure mode:** narrative build-up. A memo that reaches its ask on page two has already lost the room.

## CIO / CEO one-pager
**Lead:** the recommendation and its price in the first two sentences. **Anatomy:** recommendation + cost → why now (what changed / what it costs to wait, quantified) → the 2–3 options with one-line trade-offs → risks and the mitigation posture → the specific next decision and date. **Ceiling:** one page, hard. **Failure mode:** surviving the 90-second read but not the follow-up meeting. Every number must have a source the user can produce when asked.

## Executive email
**Lead:** BLUF, the bottom line in the subject line and the first sentence. **Anatomy:** bottom line → 2–4 tight supporting points → the explicit ask with a date. **Ceiling:** ~150 words. If it wants more, it is a memo pretending to be an email. **Failure mode:** the buried ask, an email whose recipient can't answer "what do you want from me?" after one read.

## Decision doc
**Lead:** the decision to be made and the deadline for making it. **Anatomy:** decision + deadline → context in three sentences max → options with prices and trade-offs (status quo included) → recommendation with confidence level and the one thing that would change it → who decides, who's consulted, when. **Ceiling:** 2 pages. **Failure mode:** the hidden recommendation. A decision doc that pretends to a neutrality it doesn't have reads as manipulation when discovered.

## QBR / program narrative
**Lead:** the headline of the quarter in one sentence, trajectory rather than activity. **Structure and ceiling:** owned by parvis-reviews in `skills/parvis-reviews/references/review-templates.md`. Follow that file for what the QBR contains and how long it runs, and use this entry for the craft angle when a QBR narrative is written outside the review cadence. **Register note:** one named miss builds more credibility than five polished wins. **Failure mode:** activity theater, a list of things done instead of a claim about where the program is going.

## Regulator-facing response
**Lead:** direct answer to the question asked, in the question's own framing. **Anatomy:** the answer → the evidence (specific: metric, control, test, date) → scope and boundaries of the answer stated plainly → committed follow-ups with owner and date, only where genuinely intended. **Ceiling:** as long as the question requires, not a word longer. **Register:** answer what was asked, precisely. Volunteer context only where its absence would mislead. No speculation, no aspiration presented as state, no "we believe" where "we have verified" or "we have not verified" is the truth. **Failure mode:** the accidental commitment, enthusiasm that reads as a promise, or scope-creep in an answer that invites the next exam question.

## Org announcement / staff comms
**Lead:** the why, in language a staff engineer respects, the real reason rather than the sanitized one. **Anatomy:** why → what's changing, concretely → what it means for the reader ("what changes for you Monday") → sequencing and dates → where questions go. **Ceiling:** one page. **Register:** an engineering org detects spin at parts-per-million concentrations, and plain speech about hard things is the only durable currency. **Failure mode:** corporate anesthesia, an announcement about a reorg that never says who reports to whom.

## Talking points / talk track
**Lead:** the one sentence the user must land even if the meeting collapses to two minutes. **Anatomy:** the must-land line → 3–5 points, each one spoken sentence plus its supporting number → anticipated pushback with the one-line response to each → the close (the ask restated). **Format:** fragments meant to be spoken rather than prose to be read, and nothing the user wouldn't naturally say aloud. **Failure mode:** a script. Talking points that read as an essay produce an executive reading an essay.

## Incident executive summary
**Lead:** customer impact and current state in the first sentence. **Anatomy:** impact (who, how many, how long, quantified) + current state → cause at the depth known, uncertainty stated plainly → what contained it and when → what prevents recurrence, with owner and date → what the board/CIO should take from it (pattern or one-off, and the evidence for which). **Ceiling:** one page in the first 48 hours. The full review is a different document. **Register:** calm, precise, free of defensiveness. The summary's job is trust, and trust comes from the reader sensing nothing is being managed. **Failure mode:** premature certainty about cause, which converts one incident into two credibility problems when the story changes.

## Meeting pre-read (non-board)
**Lead:** the decision this meeting must make, written as a question. **Anatomy:** the decision question → the minimum context needed to answer it and nothing beyond that → the disagreement, where one genuinely exists, presented fairly from both sides → what is explicitly NOT being decided in this meeting → what each attendee should arrive having read. **Ceiling:** 2 pages. **Register note:** a pre-read that argues one side without naming the other turns the meeting into an argument about fairness instead of the decision, and the room remembers that longer than the outcome. **Failure mode:** an unbounded scope, because a pre-read that never says what is off the table lets the room reopen settled questions and run out of clock. (The board and risk-committee pre-read has its own anatomy above. Use that one when the audience is the committee.)

## MBR narrative
**Lead:** what changed since last month, in one sentence. **Structure and ceiling:** owned by parvis-reviews in `skills/parvis-reviews/references/review-templates.md`, at 2 to 4 pages or about 10 to 12 slides. Follow that file for what the MBR contains, and use this entry for the craft angle when an MBR narrative is written outside the review cadence. **Register note:** monthly cadence is short enough that a spun month is visible the next month, so a named miss costs less than a discovered one. **Failure mode:** a shrunken copy of the QBR, the same twelve metrics with no claim about the month. The MBR reports movement, the QBR claims trajectory, and confusing the two produces a monthly document nobody reads.

## Launch / release announcement (adoption comms)
**Lead:** what the reader gets, in one line, in their terms rather than the platform's. **Anatomy:** what it is in one line → the top three concrete gains (time saved, tickets removed, compliance handled, cognitive load removed) → who it is for → how to start, where the first step is genuinely small → where help lives → what is next. **Ceiling:** one page, sized to its channel, and the channel is named before drafting. **Register note:** claims about speed, adoption, or satisfaction carry real numbers or `[X]`, never an adjective standing in for a figure. **Failure mode:** platform-internal framing. "We are pleased to announce the v2 provisioning engine" fails. "Cloud accounts in 20 minutes, compliance included" is the job.

## Value narrative (the case to a skeptical audience)
**Lead:** the situation stated in the audience's own economics. **Anatomy:** situation → impact, quantified in the reader's units → resolution with its price and its first step. Translate per audience, since a CFO reads cost per workload and avoided spend, product leadership reads lead time and cognitive load, and risk reads audit posture. **Ceiling:** one page. **Register note:** the status quo carries a stated cost rather than an implied one, and every number carries its provenance per core T2. **Failure mode:** a claim the next review cannot back, which is a credibility loan the program repays with interest.

## Success story
**Lead:** the team's before and after, in the team's own numbers. **Anatomy:** the team and what it was doing → before and after with real figures marked `[user-input]` → what the team actually did, with the team as protagonist and the platform as the tool → what a second team would need to repeat it. **Ceiling:** one page. **Register note:** peer proof beats self-praise, and no team, person, or quote appears without the user's confirmation. **Failure mode:** the platform starring in someone else's story, which readers discount instantly and which costs the relationship with the team that did the work.
