# Be human

*Skill version 2.6.0 · Last updated 2026-09-20 · Parvis release 2.6 (2026-09-20)*

A field guide, adapted from Wikipedia's *Signs of AI writing*, for prose that doesn't announce itself as machine-made. Each entry names a tell, explains why it reads as artificial, and gives the move a human writer makes instead.

## How to use this

Two modes:

1. **Drafting.** Keep these in mind as you write, so the first draft is already clean.
2. **Scrubbing.** After a draft exists, pass over it hunting for these patterns and cut or rewrite them.

The goal is not to ban words, with one deliberate exception. Punctuation follows the preferences stated in the `parvis-owner` skill, and where none are stated the default is no em dashes and few colons or semicolons (rule 13). Everything else here is fine in isolation. A human uses "however" sometimes. The tell is *density and reflexiveness*, the same move made over and over, on autopilot, whether or not the sentence needs it. When you notice yourself producing one of these by default, stop and ask whether a plainer sentence carries the meaning better. Usually it does.

One more principle worth holding onto: most of these tics are ways of adding shape without adding information. Real writing earns its emphasis. If a sentence would say the same thing with the flourish removed, remove it.

---

## 1. Inflated significance

The most common tell. The writing keeps insisting the subject matters, that it "stands as a testament," marks a turning point, leaves a lasting legacy, instead of just conveying the facts and letting the reader judge.

Watch for: *stands as / serves as a testament to, plays a vital (pivotal, crucial, significant) role, underscores its importance, highlights its significance, leaves a lasting impact, continues to captivate, cements its place, solidifies its position, a watershed moment, a defining moment, an indelible mark, enduring legacy, rich history, deeply rooted.*

- **Don't:** "The bridge stands as a testament to the city's enduring engineering legacy and continues to captivate visitors to this day."
- **Do:** "The bridge opened in 1932 and still carries about 40,000 vehicles a day."

State what happened. Significance shown is more convincing than significance asserted.

## 2. Promotional / travel-brochure tone

Prose slides into the register of marketing copy or a tourism site, admiring, glossy, selling rather than telling.

Watch for: *breathtaking, stunning, must-visit, must-see, hidden gem, nestled in the heart of, vibrant, rich cultural heritage, rich tapestry, stunning natural beauty, world-class, boasts.*

- **Don't:** "Nestled in the heart of the valley, this vibrant town boasts a rich cultural heritage and breathtaking views."
- **Do:** "The town sits in the valley and is known for its 15th-century church and weekly market."

If it sounds like a brochure, neutralize it. Concrete detail beats adjectives.

## 3. Editorializing asides

Little meta-comments that tell the reader how to feel or that something is worth noticing, which a confident writer just... doesn't do. The noticing should be built into what you chose to include.

Watch for: *it's important to note, it's worth noting, it is worth mentioning, it's important to remember, importantly, notably, interestingly, no discussion would be complete without, it should be noted that.*

- **Don't:** "It's important to note that the policy also affected small businesses."
- **Do:** "The policy also affected small businesses."

Nine times out of ten you can delete the throat-clearing and keep the sentence. The remaining tenth, rewrite so the point carries its own weight.

## 4. Filler transitions

A small set of connectors deployed on reflex to glue sentences together, whether or not the logical relationship they claim actually exists.

Watch for: *moreover, furthermore, in addition, additionally, on the other hand, in contrast, consequently, thus, hence,* and reflexive sentence-initial *however.*

- **Don't:** "The team missed the deadline. Moreover, the budget was exceeded. Furthermore, morale declined."
- **Do:** "The team missed the deadline and went over budget, and morale declined."

Vary connective tissue, or drop it. Not every sentence needs a signpost to the previous one. Often plain sequence or a single "and" or "but" reads more naturally than a formal adverb.

## 5. Reflexive summaries

Wrapping up when nothing needs wrapping up, restating in a closer what was just said, even after two paragraphs.

Watch for: *In summary, In conclusion, Overall, Ultimately, In essence, To sum up, All in all.*

- **Don't:** end a short passage with "In conclusion, the results show a clear upward trend."
- **Do:** let the last substantive sentence be the ending, or state the takeaway once, plainly, without announcing it as a conclusion.

Humans summarize in long documents where a reader might have lost the thread. They don't summarize three sentences.

## 6. Negative parallelisms

The "It's not X, it's Y" construction, and its cousins ("not only… but also," "It isn't just about A; it's about B"). It manufactures drama through contrast. AI reaches for it constantly, sometimes in back-to-back sentences.

- **Don't:** "This isn't just a phone. It's a statement." / "It's not about the money, it's about the principle."
- **Do:** "The phone's main selling point is its design." / "She objected on principle."

If you want to emphasize Y, just say Y. You rarely need to stage a fake negation of X to get there.

## 7. The rule of three

Defaulting to triplets (three adjectives, three examples, three clauses) because three *feels* complete. One triple is fine. Every list arriving in threes is the tell.

- **Don't:** "a bold, innovative, and transformative approach that streamlines, simplifies, and accelerates the process."
- **Do:** "a new approach that speeds up the process." (Use one adjective if one is true. Use four items if there are four. Break the rhythm.)

Count your groupings. When they're all threes, vary the number and cut the adjectives that are just synonyms padding the set.

## 8. False ranges

"From X to Y" phrasing that implies a spectrum when X and Y are just two loosely related things dressed up to sound comprehensive.

- **Don't:** "From intimate gatherings to global movements, the idea resonates everywhere." / "From startups to enterprises, everyone benefits."
- **Do:** "The idea appeals to both small groups and large organizations." Or name what you actually mean instead of implying a full continuum you can't back up.

## 9. Trailing "-ing" clauses that editorialize

Sentences that end with a present-participle phrase adding a layer of significance or consequence that wasn't earned, such as "…, highlighting its importance," or "…, reflecting a broader trend."

Watch for trailing: *highlighting, reflecting, emphasizing, showcasing, underscoring, ensuring, solidifying, cementing, demonstrating, further solidifying, contributing to.*

- **Don't:** "The app added dark mode, reflecting a growing demand for customization."
- **Do:** "The app added dark mode." (If the trend claim matters and you can support it, make it its own sentence with evidence. If you can't, cut it.)

These tacked-on clauses are almost always unsupported interpretation. Delete them or promote them to real, sourced statements.

## 10. Vague attribution (weasel words)

Assigning opinions or claims to an unnamed authority to sound sourced without actually sourcing anything.

Watch for: *some critics argue, observers have noted, experts say, industry reports suggest, many believe, it is widely regarded, studies show* (without the study).

- **Don't:** "Some critics argue the design is dated."
- **Do:** Name who. "In a 2024 review, *Wired* called the design dated." Or, if you don't have a source, drop the claim or state it as your own view.

If you can't say who, you probably shouldn't be attributing it to anyone.

## 11. Filler openers and padding

Throat-clearing intros that delay the actual content, and generic statements so smoothed-over they'd fit any topic.

Watch for: *In today's fast-paced world, In the ever-evolving landscape of, In an era of, When it comes to, It is a well-known fact that.*

- **Don't:** "In today's fast-paced digital world, communication is more important than ever."
- **Do:** Start with the specific thing you actually have to say. Delete the runway.

Test any sentence: could it appear, unchanged, in an article on a completely different subject? If yes, it's filler. Replace it with something only true of *this* topic.

## Formatting tells

Style and formatting give AI away as much as word choice.

## 12. Formatting overkill

Reaching for structure when a sentence would do.

- **Bold everywhere.** Key terms bolded like a textbook. Bold should be rare and deliberate. When everything is emphasized, nothing is.
- **Lists for everything.** Bulleted or numbered lists where flowing prose is clearer. Use lists for genuinely discrete, parallel items, not to chop up a paragraph that wanted to be a paragraph.
- **Bullets with bold lead-in labels.** The "**Scalability:** the system scales well" pattern, where the bold word is just restated in the sentence after it. This is one of the loudest tells. If you must list, let the bullet be a plain sentence.
- **Title Case On Every Heading.** Sentence case reads more naturally.
- **Emojis as decoration.** 🚀 ✅ 💡 in headers, bullets, or section breaks. Skip them in anything but casual chat, and even then, sparingly.

Default to prose. Add formatting only when the structure of the content genuinely calls for it, not to look organized.

## 13. Punctuation tells

- **Follow the user's punctuation preferences.** Read the Punctuation line under Communication preferences in the `parvis-owner` skill and apply it to every piece of prose written for the user. Where it states a rule, that rule wins over anything below.
- **Default when none are stated: em dashes out, colons and semicolons rare.** This keeps the system's own prose clean.
  - **Em dash (—): avoid.** Rewrite around it every time. Not occasionally, not for emphasis, not even where a style guide would call it correct. A comma, a period, or parentheses always works.
  - **Semicolon (;): avoid almost entirely.** Split the sentence in two instead.
  - **Colon (:): minimize.** Keep it only where it genuinely introduces a list or a formal definition, never as a rhythm device mid-sentence. If colons or semicolons appear more than once or twice on a page, the page needs rewriting into shorter sentences.
  - The only exceptions are text the user supplies containing these marks and direct quotes.
- **Missing en dashes for ranges.** AI tends to write ranges with hyphens ("1990-2000," "pages 5-9," "a 3-2 win") where the correct mark is an en dash (1990–2000, pages 5–9, a 3–2 win). Getting ranges right is a small human signal.
- **Curly vs. straight consistency.** Text pasted from a chatbot often mixes smart quotes and apostrophes ("" '') with straight ones in a way that's inconsistent with its surroundings. Match whatever the destination uses.

## 14. Section-template tells

Prose that snaps into a rigid, predictable scaffold regardless of subject: an introduction, then a "Challenges" section, a "Future Prospects" or "Future Outlook" section, and a "Conclusion", each present because the template has the slot, not because the content demanded it. Let the material determine the structure. If a section exists only to fill a shape, cut it.

## Chatbot residue

These are the leftover fingerprints of an assistant talking to a user. In finished writing they should never appear.

## 15. Assistant pleasantries and meta-talk

Watch for and delete:

- Sycophantic openers: *Great question! That's a fascinating topic! Absolutely! Certainly! Sure!*
- Sign-offs and offers: *I hope this helps! Let me know if you'd like… Would you like me to… Feel free to ask…*
- Self-reference: *As an AI language model… As a large language model… I'm just an AI…*
- Knowledge-cutoff hedging: *As of my last update… As of my knowledge cutoff…* (when it's irrelevant to the point).
- Unfilled placeholders left in: *[insert company name], [Your Name here], [add detail].*
- Restating the request back before answering: *Sure, here is a summary of the article you asked me to summarize…*

Finished prose is addressed to the reader, not to the person who prompted it. Strip every trace of the conversation that produced it.

## Final pass

Before calling a draft done, reread it once specifically hunting these. In practice the highest-yield checks are:

1. Cut every "it's worth noting / important to note" and see if the sentence survives (it will).
2. Find every em dash and every trailing "-ing, …" clause and rewrite each. Then count colons and semicolons, and thin them if they cluster.
3. Count your triples and your "however / moreover / furthermore." If they cluster, thin them.
4. Kill any "It's not X, it's Y" and any "In conclusion / Overall" that caps something short.
5. Ask of your opening line and any generic sentence: could this sit in an article on any other subject? If yes, make it specific or delete it.

What's left should sound like a person who knows the subject and respects the reader's time, plain where plain works, specific instead of grand, and shaped by what's actually being said rather than by a template.
